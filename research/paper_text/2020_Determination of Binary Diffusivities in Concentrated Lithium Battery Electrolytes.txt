Determination of Binary Diﬀusivities in Concentrated Lithium
Battery Electrolytes via NMR and Conductivity Measurements
Sergey Krachkovskiy,* Martin Dontigny, Sylviane Rochon, Chisu Kim, Michel L. Trudeau,
and Karim Zaghib
Cite This: J. Phys. Chem. C 2020, 124, 24624−24630 Read Online
ACCESS Metrics & More Article Recommendations
ABSTRACT: We report on the experimental determination of Onsager−Stefan−Maxwell
binary diﬀusivities using the pulsed-ﬁeld gradient nuclear magnetic resonance (PFG NMR)
technique, which are required for the concentrated solution theory to properly describe mass
transport in practical liquid electrolytes for Li batteries. The ionic conductivity, calculated
based on the obtained diﬀusivities, matches perfectly with the experimental values. We
demonstrate the eﬀectiveness of the approach using two test solutions of either lithium bis
(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl) imide or lithium bis (ﬂuorosulfonyl) imide in tetraethylene glycol
dimethyl ether in a temperature range of 20−50 °C. Accurate parametrization is achieved by reassessing the interpretation of PFG
NMR-measured ionic diﬀusion coeﬃcients. In particular, using the supporting theoretical background, we postulate that the
diﬀusion coeﬃcients represent an average of self-diﬀusion when the ions move independently and of the directionally correlated
motion when the ions interact with each other even without the formation of neutral aggregates. A proper deconvolution of these
two parts allows one to calculate the required binary diﬀusivities with high precision.
■ INTRODUCTION
Eﬀective modeling of battery performance is one of the
prerequisites for the widespread adoption of electric drive
vehicles. The operation of battery management systems relies
on the accurate parametrization of charge and mass transport
properties inside the cell and particularly in the electrolyte
domain through which ions ﬂow from one electrode to
another.1 This is especially true at extreme regimes in terms of
current and temperature when the electrolyte solutions
experience large concentration polarizations because of the
fairly low values of the Li+ cation transference number and salt
diﬀusivity.2,3 Reliable data on the mass transport properties of
an electrolyte solution as functions of both salt concentration
and temperature have become the centerpiece of para-
metrization.

model the electrolyte domain of practical electrochemical
systems. In this theory, mass transport is driven by mutual drag
forces between components. The correlation between electro-

Unfortunately, these OSM diﬀusivities are not typically
accessible by experiment. It was demonstrated though, using

self-diﬀusivities of all species in solution were measured by
pulsed-ﬁeld gradient nuclear magnetic resonance (PFG
NMR).8,9 Although the comparison of the ionic conductivity
values calculated using eqs 5 and 6 with the experimentally
measured ones showed qualitative agreement in the entire
concentration range with both having a maximum in a salt
concentration of around 1 M, the predicted values were
consistently 1.5−2.0 times higher.7 This could be explained by
the fact that PFG NMR-measured diﬀusivities cannot be
actually assigned as self-diﬀusivities in the case of concentrated
electrolytes. The concept of self-diﬀusion assumes that all
solution species move independently of each other, which is an
approximation valid only in the inﬁnitely dilute limit of
electrolyte solutions where PFG NMR indeed determines self-
diﬀusion coeﬃcients.10 On the other hand, in concentrated
electrolytes, the local electroneutrality condition ensures that
the movement of positive and negative ions from a region of
higher concentration to that of lower concentration is
correlated.11 Therefore, the concept of self-diﬀusion in
concentrated electrolyte solutions represents a considerable
simpliﬁcation of the interactions among ions and should be
used with caution.

As test samples, we chose two single-solvent lithium battery
electrolytes consisting of either lithium bis (triﬂuoromethane-
sulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) or lithium bis (ﬂuorosulfonyl) imide
(LiFSI) solution in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether
(TEGDME). Ether-based electrolytes are known for their
superior reductive stability with Li metal in comparison with
standard carbonate-based electrolytes.14−16 A substitution of
the most commonly used graphite anode by Li metal, which
has approximately 10 times higher theoretical gravimetric
capacity, is considered a very promising step toward next-
generation high-energy-density battery systems. Although there
are other parameters determining the energy density of a
battery, an increase of up to 70% in energy density can be
expected by using an Li metal anode.17,18 First, we
characterized the transport phenomena in an LiTFSI−
TEGDME (1:6 molar ratio) electrolyte in the temperature
range of 20 to 50 °C, and then compared the transport
parameters of the electrolyte to the ones of LiFSI−TEGDME
(1:6 molar ratio) solution at 50 °C. We demonstrated that in
both cases the ionic conductivity (κ) calculated using eq 5 with
the binary diﬀusivities deﬁned by eqs 7−10 perfectly matched
the experimental data. In contrast, a twofold to sixfold
diﬀerence was observed between theoretical and experimental
data when binary diﬀusivities were estimated using Darken
relations (eq 6) under the assumption that PFG NMR
provided self-diﬀusivities of species in the electrolyte.
■ EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Sample Preparation and Test Conditions. Two
solutions, LiTFSI−TEGDME and LiFSI−TEGDME (1:6
molar ratio in both cases), were prepared in an argon-ﬁlled
glove box (<0.5 ppm oxygen and water content). Battery-grade
chemicals from 3M (LiTFSI), Nippon Shokubai (LiFSI), and
Sigma-Aldrich (TEGDME) were used. The ionic conductivity
was measured in the temperature range of 20−50 °C for both
electrolytes, and the PFG NMR data were collected at 20, 25,
30, 35, 40, and 50 °C for the ﬁrst electrolyte and at 50 °C for
the second one.
Conductivity Measurements. Electrochemical impe-
dance spectroscopy data were obtained using an MCS 10
(Bio-Logic) 2-pole conductivity system with frequency scans
from 50 kHz to 100 Hz using an excitation amplitude between

the range of 1−3 ms and 100−400 ms, respectively. Diﬀusivity
values were obtained from ﬁts of the signal attenuation to the
parameters of the pulse sequence according to Stejskal’s and
Tanner’s equation9 using the Bruker Dynamics Center 2.2.1
software. The accuracy of the diﬀusion coeﬃcients measured
by PFG NMR was estimated as ± 1%, based on ﬁt quality.
■ RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Ionic conductivity values as well as 7Li, 19F, and 1H PFG NMR
data representing cation, anion, and solvent diﬀusion
coeﬃcients (D+*, D−* and D0*) respectively, for the LiTFSI−
TEGDME electrolyte as a function of temperature are listed in
Table 1.
We would like to emphasize here that although it is common
for the diﬀusion coeﬃcient measured by 7Li PFG NMR to be
termed “cation diﬀusivity,” in reality, it represents the
ensemble-averaged diﬀusion coeﬃcients of all lithium-contain-
ing species in solution, including cations (e.g., Li+), neutral ion
pairs [Li+−TFSI−], and even negatively charged ion aggregates
[Li+−(TFSI−)2]−. The same is valid for anion and solvent
diﬀusivities. Because of that, the direct utilization of PFG
NMR-measured parameters and especially their identiﬁcation
as “ionic self-diﬀusivities” is a signiﬁcant oversimpliﬁcation of
the mass transport phenomenon in concentrated battery
electrolytes. Particularly, this oversimpliﬁcation aﬀects the
estimation of the lithium transport number directly from PFG
NMR-measured diﬀusivities (τ+, eq 11)whichsimply
approaches 0.5 in the case of strong ion association and does
not fully reﬂect diﬀerences in the nature of ions.20,21

important to clarify here that α = 0.83 does not mean that 83%
of ions are involved in neutral ion aggregates and do not
contribute to conductivity, as is often interpreted in related
publications. What it does mean is that within the OSM
formalism, only 17% of the ionic conductivity is attributed to
the “self-part” related to the tracer diﬀusion coeﬃcients of
charged particles via the Nernst−Einstein equation, while the
remaining 83% are assigned to the “distinct part” associated
with the directional correlation between the motion of
diﬀerent ions.27 Under this interpretation, an increase in α
with temperature makes even more sense because while the
added thermal agitation weakens ion pairing, it increases the
probability of “collisions” (“interactions”) between ions. These
data are ensemble-averaged, implying that all species in
solution are brought into consideration with ci and cT deﬁned
by the original chemical composition of the electrolyte, while
the degree of ion interaction has an input on eﬀective diﬀusion
coeﬃcients. Following this description, one can calculate
binary OSM diﬀusivities through eqs 7−10 and all the relevant
mass transport parameters through eqs 3−5 (Table 2).
The ﬁrst thing to note from the data shown in Table 2 is a
signiﬁcant dependence of ++0 on temperature. At room
temperatures, Li+ is tightly bound to TEGDME molecules and
can be considered as a cation−solvent complex (low values of
++0). If more than one TEGDME is included into the
complex, then the cations become the bulkiest/heaviest species
in solution, hampering the eﬀectiveness of positive charge
transfer. The Li+−TEGDME residence time decreases with an
increase in the temperature, promoting the rapid exchange of
solvent molecules around Li+ (higher values of ++0), which
evidently has an impact on the Li+ mobility as it reduces the
probability of the simultaneous presence of 2 TEGDME
molecules in the Li+ solvation shell, increasing the average
lifetime of unbound Li+ with an extremely small hydrodynamic
radius, and it facilitates momentum exchange between the
complex and the more mobile, free TEGDME.27 In contrast,
+−0 stays essentially unchanged in the entire temperature
range. Probably, the added thermal energy is compensated by
the decreased dielectric constant of the solution and,
consequently, by an appearance of bulky negatively charged
triple ion aggregates [Li+ − (TFSI−)2]−. As a result, one can
observe an increase in the Li+ cation transference number with
the temperature increase shown in Figure 3. t+ reaches 0.7 at
50 °C, which is in good correlation with the prediction based
on MD simulations reported recently.27

A comparison of the experimentally measured ionic
conductivities with the theoretical values calculated within
the CST in the temperature range of 20−50 °C is shown in
Figure 4. The Darken relations (eq 6) were used in the ﬁrst

and theoretical conductivities rises from approximately 100%,
as observed previously,7 to approximately 500%, which is not
acceptable for the reliable modeling of battery performance.
This demonstrates the inﬂuence of the increased correlation in
the motion of ions in concentrated electrolytes at a high
temperature on the PFG NMR data (Figure 2) and conﬁrms
the fact that these diﬀusion coeﬃcients cannot be termed “self-
diﬀusivities” even though they are often referred to that way in
the peer-reviewed literature.28−30 The separation of the self-
part of ionic diﬀusion from the correlated motion of ions in the
electrolyte was achieved via a comparison of PFG NMR data
with the ideal ionic diﬀusivity calculated through the Nernst−
Einstein relation (eqs 7−9). The measured conductivity was
utilized within the ideal solution approximation in that case to
identify the degree of “non-ideality” of the electrolyte.
Subsequently, binary OSM diﬀusivities were deﬁned using
the obtained correction (eq 10). The resulting values of ionic
conductivity calculated according to the CST (eq 5) are
completely in line with the experimental data throughout the
temperature range (Figure 4), demonstrating the eﬀectiveness
of the suggested two-step approach consisting of the
consecutive application of the ideal and the concentrated
solution theories.
The above-described analysis was also applied to the
characterization of the LiFSI−TEGDME (1:6 molar ratio)
solution at 50 °C(Table 3) to study the inﬂuence of anions on
mass transport. The ﬁrst point to note in comparing the
experimental results of the two electrolytes is that the PFG
NMR-measured diﬀusivities of all species in solution are higher
in the case of LiFSI. This implies that the viscosity of this
liquid is lower (which can be explained by the smaller size of
the anion), and aﬀects the frequency of the solvent change in
the Li+ solvation shell. The longer residence time of the Li+−
TEGDME complex at a lower viscosity leads to a lower value
of ++0 in comparison to the one in the LiTFSI-based
electrolyte. At the same time, the decreased friction between
the anions and the solvent is represented by the higher value of
+−0. Therefore, although the LiFSI-based electrolyte possesses
better salt diﬀusivity and higher ionic conductivity than the
LiTFSI-based electrolyte, the contribution of Li+ cations to this
conductivity is signiﬁcantly less (t+(LiFSI) = 0.42 vs
t+(LiTFSI) = 0.70 at 50 °C). Finally, we would like to note
the perfect correlation between the experimental and
theoretical values of ionic conductivity as was also observed
for the LiTFSI−TEGDME solution.
■ CONCLUSIONS
We have reassessed the interpretation of PFG NMR data in
their application to the characterization of concentrated
electrolytes utilized in practical energy storage devices. First,
we can conclude that although in the peer-reviewed literature
concerned with the electrolyte study the PFG NMR-measured
diﬀusivities are often termed the “self-diﬀusivities” of ions, this
is not a correct deﬁnition of the data, as they do not actually
represent the independent motion of ions. Second, we have
discussed the deviation between PFG NMR-measured
diﬀusivities and the ideal ionic diﬀusivity calculated through
the Nernst−Einstein relations. We insist that this discrepancy
is explained not only by the presence of neutral ion aggregates
in the electrolyte, as is typically proposed, but by any
interaction between ions even without the formation of neutral
complexes, leading to their directionally correlated motion.
Utilization of these facts allows one to separate the “self-part”
related to the tracer diﬀusivity of charged particles from the
“distinct part” associated with the directional correlation
between motions of diﬀerent ions, and to calculate binary
OSM diﬀusivities required for the parametrization of mass
