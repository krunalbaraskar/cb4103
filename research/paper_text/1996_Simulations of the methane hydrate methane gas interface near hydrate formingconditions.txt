ABSTRACT 
Molecular dynamics simulations have been used to probe the interface between type I methane hydrate aud 
methane gas in the temperature range 250-300 K, and at pressures of 1 and 100 MPa. The interface was found 
to be stable up to 270 K, with the onset of melting occurring more readily at the lower pressure. Disordering of 
the surface layer was observed at all temperatures, to the extent that under some conditions a stable liquid film 
formed between the clathrate and gas phases. 
1. INTRODUCTION 
During recent years, two new major research drives have developed with respect to clathrate hydrates. The 
first of these relates to the mechanism by which clathrate hydrate crystals nucleate. This has been a natural 
development in the evolution of our understanding of this form of water: the theory for describing bulk 
thermodynamic properties is mature, although improvements have been identified[I][2], and promising 
descriptions of the kinetics of hydrate growth have been developed over the past 5-10 years[3]. This leaves 
the primary nucleation stage - i.e. the initial formation of a stable, embryonic, clathrate crystal - as the one major 
area in which our understanding is still lacking. 
Several mechanisms have been proposed for the primary nucleation process[4][5]. One aspect common 
to all these theories is a focus on the interfacial region between the bulk water and guest phases, this being the 
only region in which there will be sufficient mixing of hydrophobic guests such as methane with the water to 
form a clathrate. Unfortunately our knowledge of the behaviour of this interface is limited, with little or no 
experimental information about the thickness of the interfacial region, the concentration gradients that can be 
maintained therein, or the different static and dynamic structure to be expected between the bulk and interfacial 
aqueous regions. Experimental techniques have only recently developed to the stage where they can be used to 
probe such regions and some important data is now becoming available from techniques such as laser light 
scattering and neutron reflection[6]. However, much more information about the water/guest interface is needed 
before the nucleation mechanisms can become other than speculative. 
The second new area of interest has been in the development of kinetic inhibitors. The precise definition 
of "kinetic inhibition" is somewhat vague, but may be taken to refer to compounds that either delay the onset of 
hydrate formation, or affect the growth process (crystal size and shape) thereafter. The interest in kinetic 
inhibition has arisen largely from the discovery of anti-freeze glycopeptides in winter flounder, and the hope of 
developing analogues for hydrate inhibition. Modelling and experimental studies indicate that these glycopeptides

In this paper we present the first dynamic simulations of a clathrate surface: the interface between type I 
methane hydrate and methane gas. The simulations have been performed for a range of thermodynamic states 
around the melting point so as to identify the interfacial structure associated with near-hydrate-forming conditions. 
In the next section we give a brief description of the methodology, while the results and their implications are 
discussed in section 3. 
2. SIMULATION DETAILS 
Molecular dynamics simulations (MD) have been performed for 552 water molecules and up to 216 
methane molecules at a range of temperatures (250-300 K) and pressures (1, 100 MPa). Periodic boundary 
conditions were used in the x and y directions, but fixed boundaries were used in the z direction; z is the direction 
of the surface normal. A reflective boundary was used for the upper z limit, and a clathrate layer one unit cell 
thick (11.86 /~ for the conditions and potentials used in this study) was immobilised to create the lower z 
boundary. The use of a frozen boundary rather than periodicity in the z direction does not appear to have had 
much effect in simulations of the liquid water/ice interface[9]. The water molecules were initially arranged 
in a 2x2x3 array of unit cells (2x2x2 dynamic and 2x2xl frozen cells) exposing the (001;0) surface[8], with 
oxygen positions taken from neutron data, and hydrogens assigned in accord with normal practice[10]. 
Initially all 96 cavities in the clathrate were occupied by methane, with the remaining 120 methane molecules 
comprising the gaseous side of the interface. 
Water was modelled with the SPC potential[ll]. For pure water systems, this has been shown to give 
the same interfacial behaviour as TIP4P water, albeit at a lower temperature[12]; we therefore do not expect 
the physical properties of the present study to be strongly dependent on this choice of water potential. Methane 
was modelled by a single Lennard-Jones site, with parameters taken from the work of Jorgensen et a/.[13], 
and Lorenz-Berthelot mixing rules were used. All intermolecular interactions were neglected when the 
intermolecular separation exceeded 11.3 /~; this truncation has been found to give equivalent results to an 
EWALD method in clathrate systems[14]. 
Equilibration was achieved in several stages. Initially the entire clathrate region was held frozen, and the 
gas allowed to equilibrate in the presence of this solid boundary. A further 5 ps (2000 time steps) were then 
simulated during which time the clathrate was also allowed to move, but with retarded water dynamics (masses 
of 200 u for O and 50 u H); this allowed the gas to equilibrate around the relaxed hydrate surface, while still 
ensuring that any surface relaxation was not an artifact of the initial configuration for the gas/surface interface. 
The system was then allowed to evolve normally for a further 5 ps before a further 150 ps trajectory was 
simulated for analysis. To give some idea of the stability of the interface, the 150 ps was generated as three 
successive 50 ps trajectories: except at the highest temperature (300 K), analysis of the second and third 50 ps 
portions revealed no significant differences, indicating that the interface is stable on the MD timescale. 
Initially, simulations were performed with a computer program used in earlier hydrate simulations[10], with 
boundary conditions modified as above, and allowing constant pressure simulations via movement of the 
reflective (gas phase) boundary. Some further simulations (the final 50 ps trajectories) were performed using 
DL_POLY[15] in the [N,V,T] ensemble (Verlet leap-frog algorithm with Hoover thermostat). Several

The (001) surface of type I hydrates has a repeat unit of about 5.9 /k (i.e. half a unit cell length), with 
successive layers rotated through 90 ° . This repeating pattern is clearly evident in the normal density profiles 
(Figure 1. ) which show a repeating three-peak pattern for water in the crystalline region (e.g. between -6 ,& and -

with layer 1 being adjacent to the interface (-5.9 -0 A,). 
The density profiles indicate that our range of state points does span the clathrate/liquid coexistence line 
for this model system. At 250 K there is essentially complete crystallinity below the surface layer, and strong 
evidence of ordering within the surface layer. On the other hand, the profile at 300 K is more characteristic of 
a liquid, with no structure in the first two layers, and only minor residual spacial order in the third layer (12-18 
/k below the surface); there is also depletion of methane from layers 1-3 at this temperature. The behaviour at 
270 K is more subtle. The higher pressure system exhibits residual peaks in the surface layer, and essentially bulk 
clathrate behaviour in all other layers; at the lower pressure, however, the surface layer has become liquid-like, 
and there is degradation of the clathrate structure in layer 2. This degradation is associated with a loss of methane 
from the hydrate region, with complete loss of the first peak and partial loss of the second peak in the methane 
density profile. This is particularly interesting as it is the first simulation to observe the pressure-induced 
stabilisation that leads to the formation of clathrate hydrates in nature. 
The conclusions drawn above are also supported by plots of gas, the radial distribution functions (RDF's) 
for pairs of atoms (A,B) where both atoms are contained within the same layer; examples for layers 1 and 2 are

completely melted at 300 K. Equivalent RDF's from layers through bulk clathrate and liquid water phases were 
also calculated and found to be indistinguishable from RDF's for layer 2 of the interface at 250 K and 300 K, 
respectively. Once again, structure is more evident in the clathrate region at higher pressures. The behaviour of

Average order parameters across the interface have been depicted in Figure 3. For comparison, the 
corresponding values for the bulk hydrate are about 0.7 and 0.1 for F 4 and F3, respectively, while those for liquid 
water are 0.0 and 0.9. From Figure 3. it is apparent that layer 4 is influenced by the frozen boundary adjacent 
to it, giving higher order parameters than are found in the bulk, but order parameters for layer 3 and (at 250 K) 
layer 2 are in quantitative agreement with those for the bulk hydrate. The trends observed in Figure 3. reinforce 
the conclusions drawn from the RDF's, but with better resolution. In particular it is clear from Figure 3. that 
melting is complete within the surface layer at 270 K an 1 MPa. This would be consistent with a melting point 
just above 270 K at this pressure. Structural order is more persistent at the higher pressure, such that at 270 K 
and 100 MPa the surface layer is still more ordered than at 260 K and 1 MPa. Comparing the high and low 
pressure order parameters, we predict a melting point above 280 K for the higher pressure. These values are much 
higher than the melting point of SPC ice (ca. 200 K) and indicates that the inclusion of methane into the water 
network leads to enhanced mechanical stability compared with ice; we note that clathrate hydrates can exhibit 
very slow melting rates, which is consistent with such mechanical stability.

Mean square displacements (MSD' s) for the various atoms as a function of time have also been calculated 
for the interfacial layers; again, only portions of atomic trajectories that remain confined within the given layer 
have been considered. Separate x, y and z components for the (water) O atoms are depicted in Figure 5. for layers 
2 and 4 at 270 K; the plots for the surface layer (not shown) were diffusive, as expected from the discussion of 
structural order above. For layer 4 the MSD's are typical of stable crystals, with the plateau of 0.06-0.10 .a, 2 
indicating lattice vibrations with amplitudes of typically 0.25-0.30/~. The MSD's for layer 2 still indicate that 
the water is constrained to the vicinity of the lattice sites, but they also show the large amplitude, low frequency 
oscillations that precede melting; these oscillations were damped out under pressure. One striking feature of 
Figure 5. is the asymmetry between the x and y directions, with larger amplitude lattice vibrations being found 
in the x direction. A similar phenomenon was found in all the non-diffusive MSD's, with the x components 
consistently showing the larger displacements. This behaviour may be correlated with the structure of the 
underlying frozen clathrate boundary. Cleavage of a type I hydrate crystal does not yield a flat surface, but one 
with large well-defined channels[8]. Depending on the depth at which the crystal is created, these may be either 
in the x or in the y directions; in our simulations they lie in the x direction. Thus the larger amplitude vibrations 
in this direction are associated with water moving along these channels rather than across them. What is 
surprising is that this effect persists out to the second layer, which is 12-18 tl, away from the frozen boundary, 
and larger scale simulations are still needed to assess the significance of this long range dynamical correlation.

4. CONCLUSIONS 
In this paper we have presented the results of an MD computer simulation study of the interface between 
type I methane hydrate and methane gas at various temperatures and pressures. The interface was found to be 
stable up to about 270 K, and complete melting of the hydrate phase was found at 300 K. For the first time in 
MD simulations, pressure-induced stabilisation of a water interface was observed. For the SPC water model used 
in this work, the melting temperature was found to be close to 270 K at 1 MPa, but above 280 K at 100 MPa. 
We note that these temperatures are substantially higher than those found in simulations of SPC ice, and illustrate 
the importance of guest/water interactions in providing mechanical stability for clathrate hydrates. 
There was evidence of pre-melting under all conditions considered, with disordering of the surface layer 
even at 250 K. In most cases this led to the formation of a layer on the surface that showed a structure 
intermediate to that of the bulk hydrate and liquid water, but in one case (270 K, 1 MPa) this layer showed all 
the structural characteristics of liquid water. For all these systems the clathrate region below this layer remained 
stable throughout the simulations. 
The dynamic properties of the interface did exhibit a curious anisotropy, with lattice vibrations in the x direction 
being larger than those in the y direction (where z is the surface normal). This is believed to be associated with 
the presence of channels in the (001) surface of type I hydrates, but the effect was found to persist over a 
distance of 10-15 A. It is not clear to what extent this is an artifact of the simulation size, and larger simulations 
are in progress to assess this. In other respects the properties of clathrate-like portions of the interface were 
quantitatively consistent with those for bulk hydrates, and so this dynamic anisotropy is not expected to have 
influenced the conclusions drawn above.
