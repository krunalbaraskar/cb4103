Abstract
In this work, the hierarchical topology ring (HTR+) algorithm, an extension of the HTR
algorithm, was developed for identifying gas hydrate types, cage structures, and grain
boundaries (GBs) within polycrystalline structures. Utilizing molecular dynamics trajectories of
polycrystalline hydrates, the accuracy of the HTR+ algorithm is validated in identifying sI, sII
and sH hydrate types, hydrate grains, and GBs in multi-hydrate polycrystals, as well as clathrate
cages at GBs. Additionally, during the hydrate nucleation and growth processes, clathrate cages,
hydrate type, hydrate grains and ice structures are accurately recognized. Significantly, this
algorithm demonstrates high efficiency, particularly for large hydrate systems. HTR+ algorithm
emerges a powerful tool for identifying micro/mesoscopic structures of gas hydrates, enabling
an in-depth understanding of the formation mechanisms and properties of gas hydrates.

1. Introduction
Gashydratesaresolidstructurescomposedofwatermolecules
forming clathrate cages through hydrogen (H–) bonding with
guest molecules trapped inside. They commonly occur in
harsh environments like seafloor sediments, arctic regions,
and permafrost [1–3]. Their formation is promoted by high
pressure and low temperature conditions [4–6]. Gas hydrates
exhibit three common structures: sI, sII, and sH [7]. In natural
settings, the most prevalent form of clathrate hydrate is struc-
tural I (sI), with methane hydrate being the most significant
within this category [7]. Given methane’s importance as nat-

size on nanoscale polycrystalline hydrates, research on the
influence of hydrate grain types, size variations, and grain
boundary (GB) structures on the behavior of polycrystalline
gas hydrates is lacking. Consequently, molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations have become indispensable for investigat-

simulations, the identification of the microstructures of water-
based structures such as clathrate cages is critical for under-
standing the fundamental properties.
As a result, several algorithms for identifying clathrate
cages have been proposed. For instance, Guo et al intro-
duced the face saturated incomplete cage analysis (FSICA)
algorithm [32, 33] Nguyen and Molinero [34] developed the
CHILL+ algorithm. Mahmoudinobar and Dias proposed the
GRADE algorithm [35]. Hao et al presented the iterative
cup overlap (ICO) algorithm [36]. Lauricella et al devised a
method for identifying cage-like structures by searching for
water molecules centered on guest molecules [31]. Recently,
the hierarchical topology ring (HTR) algorithm [37] was
developed by Liu et al which has the following advant-
ages compared with other hydrate identification algorithms;
(1) unlike other algorithms that are difficult to handle large
systems, the HTR algorithm can efficiently process large
data sets by slicing the hydrate system and identifying the
cages within these segments. The detailed process of identi-
fying rings and cages through an optimized hierarchical topo-
logy method is the key to their high accuracy and effi-
ciency. This approach significantly improves the perform-
ance of the algorithm in systems with a large number of
water molecules, maintaining high accuracy without consum-
ing a large amount of computational resources. (2) HTR is
the only algorithm that can identify all types of intact cages,
including topological isomers, and this comprehensive iden-
tification capability is essential for detailed hydrate structure
analysis.
Thesealgorithms,however,canonlyidentifyclathratecage
structures or cage clusters; they are not capable of identify
hydrate types. Given that gas hydrates are polycrystalline
nature, the identification of hydrate types and GBs is cru-
cial for studying hydrate nucleation and growth, structural
transformations, evolution of GBs, structural destabilizations,
and dissociations of polycrystalline gas hydrates [38–41].
Therefore, it is vital to develop an algorithm for hydrate type
and polycrystalline hydrate identifications.
In this study, we developed the HTR-based polymorphic
hydrate identification algorithm, namely HTR+ algorithm.
HTR+ enablestheidentificationofhydratetypes,notjustcage
types. It includes the differentiation of grains in polycrystal-
line gas hydrates, the determination of GBs, and the identi-
fication of cages and ice structures at GBs. Additionally, the
HTR+ algorithm possesses an efficiency advantage in identi-
fyingpolycrystallinestructuresduetoitsoptimizationoflarge
systems.Thisadvantageisparticularlyprominentinpolycrys-
talline hydrate systems, which are larger than general nucle-
ationmodelstoensureanadequatenumberofgrainsandgrain
sizes.
2. Principles of the HTR+ algorithm
Intherealmofgashydrate,sI,sII,andsHareprevalenthydrate
types. These types of gas hydrates are uniquely distinguished
by specific cages: 51262 for sI hydrates, 51264 for sII hydrates,
and 51268 for sH hydrates. Consequently, the unique topology
of these three cages enables the determination of the hydrate
type.Theprocessofidentifyinghydratetypesinvolvesthefol-
lowing four steps, elucidated using sI hydrates as an example:
(I) applying the HTR algorithm to detect and record informa-
tion about all cage structures, including water molecule data
and the center of mass coordinates of each cage in a data-
base; (II) choosing two adjacent 51262 cages (sharing rings)
as starting points for topology, identifying all cages adjacent
to both these cages (sharing rings), and expanding the topo-
logical arrangement for sI hydrates. This expansion involves
transitioning from two 51262 cages to encompass two 51262
cages and four 512 cages; (III) validating whether the found
cages conform to the cage arrangement pattern of sI hydrates.
If conformity is established, the two 51262 cages are labeled
for topology and the neighboring cages as sI hydrates in the
cage database; (IV) initiating from the expanded two 51262
cages, followed by identifying another adjacent 51262 cages,
and repeating steps (II) and (III). These specific steps are out-
lined in figure 1.
The HTR algorithm possesses the capability to identify
cages and extract details, including water molecule IDs,
coordinates of the cage center of mass, guest molecules, and
surrounding rings within the cages. This capability facilit-
ates the topology identification of hydrate structures based
on cages. In figure 2, guest molecules are green-rendered,
while water molecules represented with coarse-graining are
red-painted. The coordinate axes in the lower left corner of
each sub-figure indicate the three-dimensional orientation of
the cage. First, an analysis of sI-type hydrate recognition is
conducted on the basis of structural topology of figure 2(a-
sI). In figure 2(a-sI), three connected cages are illustrated, in
which two cages on the right serving as adjacent upper and
lower 51262 cages. These two cages function as the starting
point for cage topology. Based on the hydrate’s structure, it
becomes evident that the adjacent rings of these two cages
can be uniquely positioned on a distinct cage. Consequently,
in figure 2(a-sI), the adjacent yellow rings allow identification
oftheuniquecage.Multiplesetsofadjacentringsbetweenthe
two neighboring cages are also evident, implying that the two
adjacent 51262 cages can topologically recognize six cages.
Figure 2(b-sI) shows the top-down view of six cages con-
nected through multiple sets of adjacent rings in figure 2(a-
sI), in which the connected bonds of 51262 and 512 cages
are blue- and yellow-rendered for clarification, respectively.
Here, the 51262 and 512 cages are denoted with ‘0’ and ‘1’
in HTR+ algorithm, respectively. As a result, the sI-type
hydrate’s topological structure (figure 2(a-sI)) is marked as
‘001001’ arrangement. Following, two another 51262 cages
are identified through the abovementioned procedure with the
two recognized 51262 cages serving as the starting point of the
topology.

3. Features by HTR+
Currently, the algorithms of recognition of hydrate structures
mainly focus on the clathrate cages, including the number
of cages, and the size of cage clusters during nucleation.
However, the microstructures such as hydrate type, grains size
and GBs in polycrystalline hydrates are not identified by these
algorithms, but can be identified by our HTR+ algorithm. In
order to assess the feasibility and accuracy of identification
of hydrate types by our HTR+ algorithm, three-dimensional
polycrystalline model of methane hydrate was constructed as
depictedinfigure3(a).Themodeliscomposedof27970water
molecules. It consists of five crystalline grains of methane
hydrate. Among them, the red, pink and orange crystalline
grains belong to sI type of clathrate hydrates; In which the
green and blue crystalline grains belong to sII and sH type
of clathrate hydrates, respectively. In figure 3(a) left image,
crystalline grains are differently rendered, where crystalline
grains belonging to the same hydrate type are not able to dif-
ferentiate. Figure 3(a) right image shows the configuration of
as-constructed polycrystalline model of methane hydrate that
is identified using our HTR+ algorithm. For clarification, sI-,
sII- and sH-type hydrates are red-, green- and blue-painted,
respectively. In comparison with figure 3(a) left image, the
five grains are clearly identified and occupy the identical posi-
tions in the model. In figure 3(a) right image, the water and
guest methane molecules located at GBs are grey-rendered
because they do not belong to any type of the three hydrate
types. GBs separating neighboring hydrate grains that belong
to identical hydrate types or different hydrate types are accur-
ately recognized.
The molecular behaviors at GBs play a crucial role in
impacting properties of gas hydrate systems, including mech-
anical properties [38, 41–43] and stability [40, 44]. In order to
evaluate the feasibility and accuracy of the HTR+ algorithm
in identifying hydrate GBs. Inspired by previous work by Wu
et al [38], a polycrystalline model of sI-type methane hydrate
with grain size of 11.2 nm subjected to uniaxial straining was
tested. Figure 3(b) shows the identified result of microstruc-
tures of the sI-type polycrystalline methane hydrate subjected
to uniaxial strain of 8% by HTR+. Note that guest molecules
are not displayed for emphasizing the clathrate cage struc-
tures. In the snapshot, sI-hydrate crystalline grains are blue-
pointed, while the clathrate cages at GBs are highlighted, in
whichdifferenttypesofclathratecagesaredifferentlycolored.
In zoomed-in configuration of figure 3(b), local GBs com-
posed of various clathrate cages are identified. In comparison
to clathrate cages in crystalline grains, the number of cages
located at the GBs that connect adjacent grains is relatively
small, leading to thin GBs, whereas the GB at junction region
of four crystalline grains exhibit the large number of clathrate
cages. In a nutshell, HTR+ algorithm is capable of identify-
ingcrystallinegrains,thehydratetypeofcrystallinegrainsand
GBs of complex polycrystalline gas hydrate in which multiple
crystalline grains, multiple hydrate types are involved.
4. Case results by HTR+
4.1. Hydrate types in nucleation and growth of gas hydrates
Homogenousnucleationandgrowthofgashydratearepreval-
ent. In MD simulations, gas hydrates homogenously nucleate
with complex microstructures, for example, they are neither
sI-, sII- nor sH-type hydrates. Figure 4 illustrates the hydrate
nucleation and growth by MD simulations. In the MD sim-
ulations, a mixture of water and methane molecules is pre-
pared with periodic boundary conditions in the three ortho-
gonaldirections.Acoarse-grainedbyoneparticleisemployed
for both water and methane molecule, and the particles’ inter-
actions are described using the SW potential [45]. The nucle-
ation and growth of gas hydrate are studied at temperature of
270 K and confining pressure of 10 MPa under NPT ensemble
using MD simulations with 250 ns, in which timestep is set
to be 2 fs. figures 4(a)–(g) shows the nucleated- and grown-
microstructures of gas hydrates that are structurally identified
bytheHTR+ algorithm,wheredifferenthydratetypesaredif-
ferently colored for clarification. This clearly illustrates the
formation and development of gas hydrates, evolving from
the small clusters to complex and stable crystal structures.
As is indicated, multiple sites of hydrate nucleation appear,
in which sI-type hydrate clusters are uniquely identified dur-

rate cages are captured, characterizing by transitioning from

itively identified during the nucleation and growth process
of gas hydrates using the HTR+ algorithm, as well as the

shown in figures 4(h)–(k). In a nutshell, our developed HTR+

4.2. Microstructures of polycrystalline hydrates subjected to
alternating shear
In natural settings, sediments-hosted polycrystalline hydrates
are commonly subjected to external loadings due to various
activity such as petro-activity, crustal movement, and so on.
Figure 5 illustrate the changes in the clathrate cages, sI- and
sII-typehydrateclustersinthepolycrystallinegashydratesub-
jected to dynamic alternating shear loads using the HTR+
algorithm. As is indicated, clathrate cages located at GBs of
polycrystallinehydrateareidentified.Polycrystallinehydrates
subjected to straining are structurally degraded due to the fol-
lowing two events. The first one is that, when polycrystalline
hydrates are mechanically loaded, water and guest molecules
located at mis-latticed GBs fast diffuse, leading to complex
formation and dissociation of clathrate cages in the vicinity
of GBs (figures 5(a)–(d)), thereby altering their macroscopic
physical properties. The second one is that when shearing is
critical,thesizeofcrystallinegrainsshrinks(figures5(e)–(h)),
suggesting the direct physical destruction of hydrate crystal-
linegrains.Basedonthisstructuralidentification,deformation
mechanisms of polycrystalline hydrate are revealed, which is
crucial for understanding and predicting the mechanical beha-
vior of gas hydrates.

5. Conclusions
In conclusion, the HTR+ algorithm as an extension of
the HTR algorithm was specifically tailored for identifying
micro/mesoscopic structures in gas hydrates. Its capability
extendsbeyondidentifyingindividualclathratecagestructures
to delineating hydrate type, crystalline grains, grain size and
GBs within polycrystalline hydrates, a feature not present in
previousalgorithms.Intermsofhydratetype,itaccuratelydis-
tinguishes between sI-, sII-, and sH-type hydrates. This is par-
ticularly crucial for understanding the structural and mechan-
ical behaviors of polycrystalline hydrates subjected to various
conditions.Inaddition,itisabletoidentifyingclathratecages,
hydrate clusters of sI-, sII-, and sH-types during the hydrate
nucleation and growth process, as well as ice structures. The
incorporationofthesystemsplittingalgorithminHTR+ signi-
ficantly improves the identification efficiency, rendering cost
time and system size linear instead of exponential, thereby
allowing for the structural identification of large hydrate sys-
tems. The HTR+ algorithm marks a significant leap forward
in structural identifications of gas hydrate system from the
molecular level.
